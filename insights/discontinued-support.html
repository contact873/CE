Discontinued is not abandoned

Equipment bought decades ago is still in service. A supplier who disappears when the model is superseded leaves the user with a machine nobody will support.

15 October 20266 min readConsolidated Engineers

Capital equipment has a long life. A crane, a tow tractor or a loader bought in the 1990s can still be doing useful work today. The manufacturer may have moved on to a new model, but the machine in the yard has not read the brochure.

This is the quiet test of a supplier. Anyone can sell the current model. The question is who answers the phone when the model is discontinued and the part is no longer in the catalogue.

The lifecycle is longer than the sales cycle

Sales teams are measured on the current range. Operators are measured on availability. The two clocks run at different speeds. A supplier that only supports what it currently sells is a supplier for the sales cycle, not the lifecycle.

The good outcome is a documented spares path that survives the model change: the part numbers, the alternate sources, the repair route and the person who owns the query.

The end of a model is not the end of the obligation.

What to ask before you buy

  • Which parts are specific to this model and which are shared with the current range?
  • What is the manufacturer's stated support period after the model is discontinued?
  • Which parts are held locally, and which are imported on demand?
  • What is the typical lead time for a critical part?
  • Who is the named contact for a spares query in year ten?

These questions are easier to ask before the order than after the failure. A supplier who cannot answer them is telling you something useful.

The repair route

Some parts can be repaired to the manufacturer's standard. Some cannot. The difference matters, because an unofficial repair can introduce a failure that the maintenance record will not show.

Ask for the approved repair route and the documentation that comes with it. If a part is no longer available, ask whether an approved alternative exists and who signs it off. The answer should be in writing, with a part number and a traceability document.

Planning for obsolescence

Obsolescence is not a surprise. It is a schedule. A fleet manager can plan for it by recording the critical parts, the lead times and the age of each unit, and by reviewing the list before the next failure.

The review does not need to be complicated. A spreadsheet with the unit, the critical part, the current source and the lead time is enough to start. The point is to know where the risk sits before it becomes an outage.

The supplier's side

For the supplier, supporting discontinued equipment is a discipline. It means keeping the old part numbers, the drawings and the contacts, and being willing to spend time on a low-value order because the machine matters to the user.

That is the business we are in. The equipment is the beginning of the relationship, not the end of it.
