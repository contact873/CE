Public procurement

Why public procurement fails before the tender

Most public equipment contracts are won or lost in the indexing and specification stage, long before the tender is published.

22 October 20268 min readConsolidated Engineers

Public procurement is often described as a tender process. In practice, the decisive work happens earlier: the product has to be indexed, the specification has to be written in a way the market can answer, and the budget has to survive the timetable. A tender is the visible part of a longer chain.

Indexing is not paperwork

In Pakistan, a new product may need to be indexed with the civilian departments and the Ministry of Defence before it can be introduced to a user unit. That is not a formality at the end of the process. It is the gate that decides whether the product can be demonstrated, procured and accepted.

A supplier who treats indexing as an administrative step discovers the problem when the user unit is ready and the product is not. The programme slips, and the slippage is rarely attributed to the missing index.

A tender is a deadline. Indexing is the work that makes the deadline possible.

Specification drift

Public specifications are written to be neutral, but neutrality is difficult. A specification that is too broad attracts offers that cannot be compared. One that is too narrow excludes the competition and may be challenged.

The useful middle ground is a specification written around performance and interface, with the mandatory standards named. What must the equipment do, in what conditions, with what documentation, and what must it connect to? Those questions produce a tender that can be evaluated.

The documentation burden

A public contract asks for more than a price. It asks for the registration, the tax position, the technical literature, the compliance statements, the delivery schedule, the warranty and the after-sales commitment. Each document has a format and a deadline.

A supplier who starts assembling the pack after the tender is published is already late. The pack should be a standing asset: current, indexed and ready to be adapted to the particular notice.

The timetable is part of the specification

Delivery dates in public procurement are often fixed before the supplier is chosen. The equipment may have an import lead time, a commissioning period and a training requirement. If those are not reflected in the timetable, the contract starts with an impossible date.

Ask for the realistic delivery position at the quotation stage, in writing. A supplier who promises an impossible date to win the tender is transferring the risk to the project, not removing it.

What a good bid team does

  • Maintains the registrations and the standard document pack in date.
  • Indexes new products before they are offered to a user unit.
  • Writes the specification around performance, standards and interfaces.
  • States the delivery position and the dependencies honestly.
  • Plans demonstration, commissioning and training as part of the contract.
  • Keeps the after-sales record, because the next tender will ask for it.

The tender is a test of preparation. The preparation is the procurement.
