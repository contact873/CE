The hidden cost of a late commissioning

A machine that arrives without commissioning, training or a signed handover is not an asset yet. It is a claim waiting to happen.

5 November 20267 min readConsolidated Engineers

Procurement teams measure the success of a capital purchase at the point of delivery. Operations teams measure it at the point the equipment starts earning. Between those two points sits commissioning, and it is where schedules quietly disappear.

A tow tractor that arrives on the apron is not in service. It needs a pre-delivery inspection, a commissioning run, operator familiarisation, a handover record and a spares path. Each of those is a small task. Together they decide whether the machine is available next month.

Delivery is not handover

The delivery note proves the crate arrived. The handover record proves the equipment works to specification, the operator has been trained and the site has accepted the unit. Those are different documents, and only the second one protects the buyer.

A useful handover record includes the serial number, the configuration, the commissioning results, the name of the person who accepted the unit and the date. It also includes the outstanding items, because there are always outstanding items.

An unsigned handover is an open-ended warranty discussion.

The training gap

Operator training is often treated as an optional extra. It is not. A machine used outside its rated capacity, or maintained without the correct procedure, will fail earlier and cost more. The training is part of the specification, not a favour after the invoice.

Ask for training on the actual unit, at the actual site, with the people who will run it. A generic session in a classroom is better than nothing, but it does not cover the apron layout, the blind spots or the local operating rules.

Commissioning is a schedule item

Commissioning needs time on the project programme, not a slot after the handover meeting. The site needs power, access and a competent person available. The manufacturer may need a specialist. The operator may need to be released from shift.

If those dependencies are not in the programme, commissioning slips. The equipment sits idle, the project claims delay, and the supplier and the buyer spend the next month exchanging letters about whose fault it is.

The spares path

Commissioning is also the moment to confirm the spares path. Which parts are consumables, which are critical, what is held locally and what has to be imported. A machine that stops for a filter is as unavailable as one that stops for a gearbox.

Ask for the recommended spares list at handover, not after the first failure. Ask how discontinued models are supported. Ask who owns the part number and how long the lead time is. These are simple questions with expensive answers when they are asked late.

What good looks like

  • Commissioning is in the contract and the programme, with named responsibilities.
  • The handover record is signed by the person who will operate the equipment.
  • Training happens on the unit, on site, before acceptance.
  • The spares list and the first-line maintenance schedule are handed over with the manual.
  • The supplier remains the named contact for the life of the machine.

The cheapest commissioning is the one that is planned. The most expensive is the one that is discovered.
