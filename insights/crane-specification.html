Cranes and lifting

What a crane specification should actually say

Load charts, radii and ground bearing are where most crane briefs fail. A practical guide to writing a specification that can be priced, erected and delivered.

10 November 20268 min readConsolidated Engineers

A crane enquiry that begins with a capacity in tonnes is not yet a specification. It is a wish. The capacity is the answer to a set of conditions that have to be written down first: what is being lifted, how far from the centre of the crane, how high, how often, and what the machine will stand on.

When those conditions are missing, every supplier prices a different crane. The quotations look comparable because they share a headline number. They are not comparable at all.

Start with the lift, not the machine

Write down the load, including the rigging, the spreader and anything that hangs from the hook. Then write the radius at which the load must be placed, not the radius at which the crane will sit. A load chart is a map of capacity against radius and height, and the useful figure is always the one at the working radius.

Do the same for the height. The hook height is not the same as the building height. Add the clearance above the load, the sling length and any obstruction between the hook and the landing point.

The four numbers that decide the machine

  • Load: the gross load at the hook, not the net weight on the drawing.
  • Radius: the distance from the slewing centre to the load, at the worst position in the lift.
  • Height: hook height plus clearance, under the boom or jib configuration you will actually use.
  • Duty: how many lifts, how often, and whether the crane works continuously or intermittently.

Those four numbers eliminate most of the catalogue. What remains is a short list of machines that can do the job, and a much more useful conversation about erection, foundations and site access.

A crane quotation without a load chart is a price for a photograph, not for a machine on your site.

Ground bearing is part of the specification

The crane does not stand on the drawing. It stands on a slab, a road, a pile mat or made ground. The outrigger or track pressure has to be checked against what is actually there, and the temporary works to spread that load have to be in somebody's scope.

This is the most common gap in a brief. The crane is specified, the foundation is assumed, and the first week on site is spent discovering that the ground will not take the pressure. Ask for the maximum outrigger load and the bearing pressure at the worst configuration, then confirm who is responsible for the mat or the slab.

Erection, climbing and dismantling

A tower crane is not delivered. It is erected, climbed and eventually dismantled, and each of those operations needs space, a mobile crane and a competent crew. On a dense urban site, the erection plan can decide the crane as much as the load chart does.

Put the sequence in the brief: the position of the crane, the mobile crane needed to erect it, the climbing stages, the tie-in levels and the dismantling route. If the site cannot support the sequence, the crane is wrong even if the chart says it is right.

What to put in the brief

  • The lift plan, including the worst case rather than the typical case.
  • Load, radius and height at that worst case.
  • Ground conditions and the temporary works assumption.
  • Erection, climbing and dismantling constraints.
  • Site access, delivery route and lay-down space.
  • Operator training, commissioning and handover requirements.
  • The documentation you expect at the end: load charts, certificates, inspection records and the manual.

A specification written this way is longer than a capacity figure, but it is the difference between a quotation you can compare and a delivery you can hold someone to.

The test of a good specification

Give the brief to two suppliers and ask each to return the load chart for the proposed configuration. If the charts show the same working radius, the same hook height and the same ground pressure, you are comparing like with like. If they do not, the conversation is not about price yet.

The equipment is the easy part. The conditions around it are the work.
