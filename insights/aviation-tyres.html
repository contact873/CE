Buying aviation tyres without a part number

A tyre is not a commodity. Part numbers, traceability and storage decide whether the right unit reaches the aircraft.

29 October 20266 min readConsolidated Engineers

An aircraft tyre looks simple from the outside. It is a highly controlled part, and the control starts with the part number. Buy the wrong number and the tyre may fit the wheel but not the approved configuration. Buy without traceability and the maintenance organisation cannot sign it into service.

The part number is the specification

Aircraft tyres are identified by manufacturer part number, size, load rating and speed rating. The aircraft maintenance manual will name the approved tyre and, often, the approved wheel assembly. The part number is the link between the two.

A generic size is not enough. Two tyres can share a nominal size and differ in load rating, tread or construction. The approved configuration is the one in the manual, not the one that looks closest on the shelf.

The cheapest tyre is the one that is approved, traceable and in the right condition when the aircraft needs it.

Traceability from manufacture to fit

Aviation tyres carry a serial number and a manufacturing date. The maintenance organisation needs the documentation that connects the tyre to the approved source, the storage history and the fitment record.

Ask for the certificate or release document with the delivery. Keep the storage record. A tyre that has been stored badly, exposed to heat or ozone, or mounted and removed incorrectly is not the same as one that has been kept properly, even if the serial number is the same.

Storage is part of the purchase

Tyres are not inert. They age. Heat, sunlight, ozone and certain solvents accelerate that ageing. A store that is convenient but hot is a false economy.

Keep tyres clean, dry, out of direct sunlight and away from electric motors and solvents. Rotate stock so the oldest approved tyre is used first. Record the date of receipt and the date of fitment. Those records are what an audit will ask for.

Retread and repair

Retreading and repair are controlled processes. Not every tyre is eligible, and not every damage is repairable. The approved manual and the manufacturer's instructions decide. A repair that is not approved is a risk the maintenance organisation carries.

Ask the supplier what the approved repair and retread route is for the tyre you are buying, and whether the documentation will support it. The answer should be specific, not a general assurance.

A practical buying checklist

  • The approved part number, taken from the aircraft manual.
  • The manufacturer and the release document.
  • The serial number and manufacturing date.
  • The storage history and the condition on receipt.
  • The approved fitment and removal procedure.
  • The repair and retread route, in writing.
  • The lead time for a repeat order, and the local stock position.

None of this is exotic. It is the normal discipline of a controlled part. The failure mode is treating a tyre like a consumable from a catalogue, and discovering the gap at the aircraft rather than at the desk.
